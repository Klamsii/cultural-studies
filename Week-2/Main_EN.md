## 1. Seminar 2.1: Four Global Cultural Codes

### 1.1 Defining Cultural Code
A **Cultural Code** represents a fundamental category in modern culturology and semiotics. It is an encoded matrix of signs, symbols, value systems, mental stereotypes, and semantic structures through which a specific society preserves its historical experience, transmits it across generations, and decodes incoming information from the surrounding world. 

The cultural code determines a nation's unique mindset, shapes national character, sets boundaries of acceptable behavior, and constructs a specific worldview. Without mastering the cultural code, an individual cannot fully interpret the meanings embedded in art, traditions, rituals, and language.

---

### 1.2 Preliterate (Traditional) Cultural Code
The preliterate, or traditional, cultural code represents the primary historical state of human civilization in which knowledge transmission occurred entirely without written scripts.

**In-Depth Prose Analysis:**
The primary carriers and mediums of information in preliterate culture are spoken word, mythic epics, traditional rituals, body symbolism, ceremonial dances, and ornamental tattoos. Because no physical written texts existed, culture was sustained entirely within collective human memory.

The transmission mechanism relies on direct physical contact between individuals. Knowledge is passed from elders to younger generations through oral storytelling, epic performance (such as Kazakh *zhyrs* and *tolghau*), shared ritual participation, and master-apprentice craftsmanship. Elders, shamans, and epic singers (*akyns* and *zhyraus*) function as living human libraries and guardians of tradition.

The cognitive style of preliterate culture is primarily auditory, image-based, emotional, and collective. People perceive the world not through linear book logic, but through rhythmic resonance, mythic metaphors, and emotional immersion in shared rituals. Knowledge exists only while spoken or performed in community.

---

### 1.3 Written (Book) Cultural Code
The written cultural code emerged with alphabetic scripts and reached maturity after the 15th-century Gutenberg revolution of the movable type printing press.

**In-Depth Prose Analysis:**
The foundational mediums of this era are letters, manuscripts, charters, and printed books. The invention of writing liberated human memory from holding all cultural data, delegating preservation to physical media — paper and library archives.

The operational mechanisms of the written code depend on formal schooling, university systems, scientific treatises, codified legal statutes, and state archives. Information is transmitted through reading, individual textual analysis, and systematic study.

The cognitive style of book culture creates the "Gutenberg Galaxy" (Marshall McLuhan). It is strictly linear, analytical, logical, and individually reflective. Reading requires silence, solitude, abstract thinking, and the ability to construct cause-and-effect logical chains. The written code produced modern science, critical philosophy, and the rule of law.

---

### 1.4 Screen Cultural Code (Cinema and TV)
The screen cultural code formed during the 20th century with cinema, photography, and mass television, marking a shift from printed text to dynamic audiovisual imagery.

**In-Depth Prose Analysis:**
The main mediums are film reels, television broadcasts, video recordings, and photographs. Culture comes to be measured by millions of broadcast viewers and visual frames rather than pages read.

The transmission mechanism rests on centralized mass broadcasting. Media corporations, film studios, and television networks broadcast a unified audiovisual stream to millions of viewers simultaneously, setting global standards for fashion, behavior, and political perception.

The cognitive style of screen culture is visual, sensory, synthetic, and passive. Screens combine sound, motion, and color, affecting viewer consciousness faster than text. Viewers receive pre-packaged visual worldviews, reducing individual imaginative exertion while heightening emotional empathy.

---

### 1.5 Digital Cultural Code (Interactive Networked Code)
The digital cultural code defines contemporary civilization, triggered by the Internet, social media, virtual reality, and Artificial Intelligence algorithms.

**In-Depth Prose Analysis:**
The mediums of digital culture are online platforms, hypertext, software code, mobile applications, social networks, and virtual spaces.

The transmission mechanism differs fundamentally from television broadcasting: the boundary between creator and audience collapses. Every individual becomes a **prosumer** — simultaneously consuming and producing content. Information spreads non-linearly through hyperlinks, retweets, recommendation algorithms, and viral networking.

The cognitive style of the digital code is clip-based, non-linear, mosaic-like, and multitasking. Users switch rapidly between browser tabs, consume short visual-textual blocks (memes), query search algorithms, and interact continuously with interfaces.

## 2. Seminar 2.2: Anatomy of Spiritual Culture: 6 Forms of Religious Beliefs

**Theoretical Origin:** Sir Edward Burnett Tylor (*Primitive Culture*, 1871).

### 2.1 Sir Edward Burnett Tylor's Evolutionary Theory
Tylor established the evolutionary framework in religious studies, demonstrating that human belief systems evolved from early attempts to explain nature into complex philosophical doctrines. He identified animism as the primordial form of religion.

### 2.2 Animism
Animism is the ancient belief that all nature is living and indwelt by spirits. Early humans believed mountains, rivers, trees, winds, and animals possessed souls. Tylor explained animism as an attempt to comprehend dreams, hallucinations, and death.

### 2.3 Fetishism
Fetishism involves religious worship of inanimate physical objects (*fetishes*) believed to hold supernatural, magical, or protective powers. Unlike animism which worships a spirit, fetishism venerates the physical item itself — a sacred stone, charm, or Kazakh **Tumar** amulet.

### 2.4 Totemism
Totemism is a belief system based on sacred blood kinship between a clan and an animal or plant species (*totem*). In Kazakh nomadic tradition, venerating the Sacred Steppe Wolf (**Böri**) exemplifies totemism as a symbol of ancestral warrior spirit.

### 2.5 Pantheism
Pantheism identifies God directly with nature. God is not separate from the world, but immanent within it. Ancient Tengriism reflects pantheism, viewing the Great Blue Sky (**Tengri**) and Earth-Water (**Jer-Su**) as a living divine cosmos.

### 2.6 Deism
Deism emerged during the Enlightenment (Voltaire, Newton). Deists compared God to a Supreme Clockmaker who created the physical laws of the universe but does not intervene through miracles.

### 2.7 Monotheism
Monotheism represents faith in One Transcendent God (Islam, Christianity, Judaism).

## 3. Seminar 2.3: Formation of Morality & Moral Paradoxes

### 3.1 The Golden Rule of Morality
> "Do unto others as you would have them do unto you" (*Өзіңе тілемейтінді өзгеге тілеме*).

### 3.2 Paradox 1: Absoluteness vs. Contextual Relativism
Moral commandments claim absolute universality ("Thou shalt not kill"), yet practical application depends on historical context (military defense, self-defense).

### 3.3 Paradox 2: Conscience Autonomy vs. External Social Shame (*Uyat*)
True morality demands internal conscience. However, culture frequently enforces norms through external social shame (**Uyat**) and peer pressure.

### 3.4 Paradox 3: The Trolley Problem
- **Utilitarian Ethics (Bentham, Mill):** Mandates flipping the switch to save 5 lives at the cost of 1 (math of utility).
- **Kantian Deontology:** Forbids flipping the lever, as active intervention turns the actor into a murderer, violating the categorical imperative.

## 4. Lecture 2.4: Structuralism & Semiotics: Saussure, Peirce, Morris, Vygotsky & Kristeva

**AITU Lecture 2.1:** `hrVHaXP6vj8`

### 4.1 Ferdinand de Saussure
Saussure established structural linguistics, proving that a linguistic sign consists of two inseparable parts: **Signifier (*Signifier* — acoustic image)** and **Signified (*Signified* — mental concept)**. The connection is arbitrary (*l'arbitraire du signe*).

### 4.2 Charles Sanders Peirce
Peirce formulated the sign triad: **Icon** (visual resemblance), **Index** (causal proof, smoke/fire), and **Symbol** (social convention).

### 4.3 Three Dimensions of Semiotics (Charles Morris)
1. **Syntactics:** Relations of signs to other signs.
2. **Semantics:** Relations of signs to objects.
3. **Pragmatics:** Relations of signs to human interpreters.

### 4.4 Lev Vygotsky & Julia Kristeva
Vygotsky demonstrated sign mediation in psychological development. Julia Kristeva introduced **semanalysis** as a critique of meaning production alternative to Saussurean semiology.

---

## 5. Lecture 2.5: Roland Barthes: Mythologies & Second-Order Systems

In *Mythologies* (1957), Roland Barthes demonstrated that myth is a second-order semiotic system:
- **Denotation:** Literal linguistic meaning.
- **Connotation (Myth):** Second-order ideological meaning.
- **Function of Myth:** Naturalizing bourgeois ideology into "natural common sense."

## 10. 🔥 Strict Oral Defense Master Block: 10 Comprehensive Exam Questions & Answers

#### ❓ Q1: Explain Denotation vs Connotation in Barthes's semiotics.
**Answer:**  
Denotation is the first-order literal linguistic sign. Connotation (Myth) is the second-order ideological layer where the first sign becomes a formal signifier for a secondary political meaning.

#### ❓ Q2: What is Lotman's Semiosphere?
**Answer:**  
The Semiosphere is the unified semiotic continuum of culture required for any language to exist and generate meaning. Culture operates as a collective intellect.

#### ❓ Q3: Detail Charles Morris's 3 dimensions of semiotics.
**Answer:**  
1. Syntactics (sign to sign relations), 2. Semantics (sign to object relations), 3. Pragmatics (sign to interpreter relations).

#### ❓ Q4: Distinguish Conventional Signs from Indexes.
**Answer:**  
Indexes possess a direct physical/causal proof to their referent (smoke indicates fire). Conventional signs rely purely on social contract (STOP sign).

#### ❓ Q5: Detail Tylor's animistic origin of religion.
**Answer:**  
Tylor posited animism (belief in souls indwelling nature) as the original religious form, emerging from early attempts to comprehend dreams and death.

#### ❓ Q6: Detail Utilitarianism vs Deontology in the Trolley Problem.
**Answer:**  
Utilitarianism (Bentham) mandates flipping the switch to save 5 at the cost of 1. Deontology (Kant) forbids the act, as active intervention turns the actor into a murderer.

#### ❓ Q7: What was Vygotsky's contribution to semiotics?
**Answer:**  
Vygotsky showed that higher psychological processes develop not merely biologically, but through sign mediation and cultural tools.

#### ❓ Q8: Trace the pentagram symbol in Langdon's lecture.
**Answer:**  
Pagan Venus symbol → Christian 5 wounds of Christ → Hollywood satanic trope.

#### ❓ Q9: What is the significance of the Tamgaly Sun Deity?
**Answer:**  
Petroglyphs in Tamgaly (*Kunhan*) reflecting ancient Eurasian solar mythologies and Sky worship (*Tengri*).

#### ❓ Q10: Differentiate Preliterate and Digital cultural codes.
**Answer:**  
Preliterate relies on oral memory and ritual; Digital relies on interactive networks, non-linear hypertext, and algorithmic multitasking.

## 11. 🏆 Advanced Exam Strategy & Primary Source Glossary for Topic 2

### 11.1 Primary Theoretical Citations & Primary Literature Excerpts:
- **Ferdinand de Saussure (*Course in General Linguistics*, 1916):** "Language is a system of signs that express ideas, and is printable to a science of semiology. A linguistic sign unites not a thing and a name, but a concept and a sound-image. The bond linking the signifier to the signified is arbitrary."
- **Roland Barthes (*Mythologies*, 1957):** "Myth does not hide anything and its function is to distort, not to make disappear. Myth is speech stolen and restored. It transforms history into nature, presenting politically constructed values as eternal common sense."
- **Yuri Lotman (*Universe of the Mind*, 1990):** "The semiosphere is the semiotic space necessary for the existence and functioning of languages, not a sum of individual texts. Outside the semiosphere, there can be neither communication nor language."
- **Charles Sanders Peirce (*Collected Papers*):** "A sign, or representamen, is something which stands to somebody for something in some respect or capacity. An index is a sign which refers to the Object that it denotes by virtue of being really affected by that Object."

### 11.2 Key Analytical Matrix for Exam Defense:
1. Always identify the **Signifier** (sound/visual form) vs. **Signified** (mental concept).
2. Determine if the sign is an **Icon** (resemblance), **Index** (causal link), or **Symbol** (convention).
3. Evaluate second-order **Connotation** to uncover underlying ideology in media texts.
