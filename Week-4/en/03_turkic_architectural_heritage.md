## 🎯 Essence in 60 Seconds
- **Core idea:** Turkic architecture is the translation of nomadic form into permanent material. The yurt supplied the dome and the circular plan; Islam supplied the portal, the mihrab, and the ornamental programme.
- **Characteristic features:** a dome on an octagonal drum, a monumental entrance portal (**peshtaq**), stalactite vaulting (**muqarnas**), glazed tilework, geometric and vegetal ornament, calligraphy in place of images of living beings.
- **The principal monument:** the **Mausoleum of Khoja Ahmed Yasawi** in Turkestan (built by order of Timur, late 14th century, UNESCO World Heritage Site).
- **Other monuments:** the **Aisha Bibi** and **Karakhan** mausolea in Taraz; the settlement of **Otrar**.
- **Modern Kazakhstan:** Khan Shatyr, the Hazrat Sultan Mosque, Baiterek — the tent, the dome, and ornament reinterpreted in new materials.

## 📖 Main Content

### 1. Characteristic Features of Turkic Architecture
**Turkic building is recognisable by the combination of dome, portal, and ornament standing in for imagery.**
Its key markers: a hemispherical **dome** on an octagonal drum; a monumental entrance **portal** (peshtaq) that rises above the building itself and defines the ceremonial façade; **muqarnas**, honeycomb vaulting that solves the engineering problem of moving from a square base to a round dome; facing in **glazed tile** of blue and turquoise; and **ornament** — geometric (girih), vegetal (islimi), and calligraphic. Images of people and animals are absent from religious architecture: pattern and inscription take their place.
**Example:** blue and turquoise tile is not merely decorative — it is the colour of the Sky, a continuity with the Tengriist colour semiotics of Week 3.

### 2. The Link Between Nomadic Culture and Architecture
**Turkic monumental architecture grew out of the form of the yurt rather than against it.**
The connection is visible at several points at once. The dome repeats the form of the yurt's domed roof, and the circular or centralised plan repeats its round base. The principle whereby the main space is a single hall beneath a dome reproduces the organisation of the yurt, where all life proceeds in one room around the hearth. Finally, the very idea of a vertical directed skyward — the shanyraq as an opening toward the Sky, the dome with its lantern — preserves the Tengriist "earth to Sky" vertical.
**Example:** a kumbez mausoleum on the steppe reads as "a yurt built to last forever": the same form, but in stone, because the deceased no longer migrates.

### 3. The Influence of Islam
**Islam did not abolish Turkic form; it gave that form a new function and a new decorative language.**
With the adoption of Islam new building types appear: the mosque, the madrasah, the minaret, the caravanserai, the saint's mausoleum. A compulsory orientation appears as well — the **mihrab** indicates the qibla, the direction of Mecca, so the building acquires a rigid spatial anchoring the yurt never had. The prohibition on depicting living beings in religious contexts channelled all artistic effort into ornament and calligraphy, which explains their exceptional sophistication.
**Example:** a Qur'anic inscription in Kufic script running around a portal works simultaneously as decoration, as text, and as a mark of belonging to the Islamic world.

### 4. The Principal Monuments
**The Mausoleum of Khoja Ahmed Yasawi is the central monument tying together every theme of the week.**
It was built in **Turkestan** by order of **Timur (Amir Temur)** in the late 14th century over the grave of the Sufi sheikh Khoja Ahmed Yasawi and is inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List. The building combines the largest brick dome in Central Asia, a monumental portal, a system of more than thirty rooms, and the central hall, the **kazandyk**, housing the bronze **Tai Kazan**, a cauldron for ritual water. The monument is deliberately unfinished: work stopped with Timur's death, and the open brickwork of the entrance portal remains visible to this day.
Other key sites: the elegant **Aisha Bibi** mausoleum (11th–12th c., near Taraz), famous for its carved terracotta decoration; the **Karakhan** mausoleum in Taraz; and the settlement of **Otrar**, birthplace of Al-Farabi and a node of the Silk Road.

### 5. Preservation and Reinterpretation in Modern Kazakhstan
**The heritage operates today in two modes: as a protected monument and as a living language of new architecture.**
Protection means UNESCO status, restoration, museumification, and archaeological reserves (Otrar, Tamgaly). Reinterpretation means contemporary buildings quoting traditional form: **Khan Shatyr** repeats the silhouette of a tent, the **Hazrat Sultan** mosque in Astana reproduces the classical scheme of dome plus portal plus minarets, and **Baiterek** draws on the mythological image of the World Tree. Practical means of preservation include scientific restoration rather than replica-building, digital 3D documentation, integration of monuments into tourist routes, and training in traditional crafts and conservation.

## ⚖️ Common Misconceptions

| Concept A | Concept B | Key Difference | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Peshtaq (portal)** | **Mihrab** | External ceremonial entrance vs internal niche indicating Mecca. | The portal is outside; the mihrab is inside. |
| **Mausoleum** | **Mosque** | Funerary structure over a grave vs building for collective prayer. | Yasawi mausoleum vs Hazrat Sultan mosque. |
| **Muqarnas** | **Ornament** | Three-dimensional honeycomb vault structure vs flat surface pattern. | Muqarnas is architecture; girih is decoration. |
