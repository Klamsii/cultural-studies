### 2.1 Ancient Roman Etymology: The Latin Root *Colere* and *Agri Cultura*
The word "culture" is one of the most foundational terms in the humanities. It originates from the ancient Latin verb **colere**, which carried primary practical meanings in ancient Roman agrarian society: "to cultivate the soil," "to till," "to inhabit," "to care for," and "to honor."

The original literal meaning of the Latin phrase **agri cultura** was **"cultivation of the soil" / "agriculture" (*cultivation*)**. In antiquity, culture did not designate fine arts, literature, or museum galleries; it referred strictly to the practical agronomic process of human intervention into raw nature to grow crops and harvest food. Soil left untouched remained wild (*silva*); soil tilled by human labor became *cultura*.

### 2.2 Classical Antiquity: Marcus Tullius Cicero & *Cultura Animi* (45 BCE)
The pivotal transition of the term from agricultural practice into the realm of human spirit, mind, and philosophy occurred in the 1st century BCE through the work of the Roman orator, statesman, and philosopher **Marcus Tullius Cicero (106–43 BCE)**.

In his celebrated philosophical dialogue **"Tusculan Disputations" (*Tusculan Disputations*, 45 BCE)**, Cicero adapted the agronomic phrase into a famous philosophical metaphor:

$$\text{Cultura Animi Virtus Est} \quad \text{(Philosophy is the cultivation of the soul)}$$

**In-Depth Academic Analysis of Cicero's Metaphor:**
Cicero established a direct analogy between uncultivated land and the uneducated human mind: just as even naturally fertile land cannot produce a fruitful harvest without tilling and care, so the human soul remains crude, unrefined, and ignorant without "cultivation" (*cultura animi*) through philosophy, science, poetry, and ethical instruction. Through Cicero's metaphor, culture came to define the conscious process of human self-improvement, intellectual refinement, and spiritual growth.

### 2.3 Enlightenment Era: French & German Traditions (Voltaire, Montesquieu, Rousseau, Herder)
During the 18th-century Enlightenment in France and Germany, "culture" developed into an autonomous noun denoting the degree of human rationality, moral refinement, legal progress, and social advancement:
- French philosophers (**Voltaire, Charles de Montesquieu, Jean-Jacques Rousseau**) viewed culture as the triumph of human reason over medieval superstition, religious dogma, and primitive barbarism. In French intellectual circles, "culture" and "civilization" were used nearly synonymously.
- German philosopher and humanist **Johann Gottfried Herder** in his seminal work *Ideas on the Philosophy of the History of Mankind* (1784–1791) made a revolutionary contribution by speaking of "cultures" in the plural (*Kulturen*). Herder argued that there is no single universal European standard of culture; every nation (Germans, Arabs, Chinese, Eurasian nomads) possesses its own unique national culture conditioned by geography, language, and historical development.

### 2.4 Anthropological Foundation: Sir Edward Burnett Tylor (1871)
British ethnographer and founding figure of cultural anthropology **Sir Edward Burnett Tylor (1832–1917)**, in his landmark book **"Primitive Culture" (*Primitive Culture*, 1871)**, formulated the first formal academic definition of culture:

> **Tylor's Academic Definition (1871):**  
> "Culture, or Civilization, taken in its wide ethnographic sense, is that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, law, morals, custom, and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society."

**Two Fundamental Academic Takeaways from Tylor's Definition:**
1. **Non-Biological Transmission:** Culture **is not transmitted through biological inheritance or genetics**. Humans are not born with culture; they acquire, learn, and absorb it through living in a social environment — a process known as **enculturation** (*enculturation*).
2. **Systemic Complex:** Culture encompasses far more than high art or formal education; it includes everyday habits, moral codes, legal systems, customs, and beliefs shared by society.

### 2.5 Kroeber & Kluckhohn Classification: Behavioral Definition (*Shared, learned behavior*)
American anthropologists **Alfred L. Kroeber and Clyde Kluckhohn** published a major critical review in 1952 analyzing over 160 definitions of culture. In their typology, the **behavioral definition (*behavioral definition*)** stands out as a core benchmark:

> **Kroeber & Kluckhohn's Behavioral Definition:** Culture is "shared, learned human behavior, a way of life."

### 2.6 International Benchmark: UNESCO Declaration (Mexico, 1982)
The World Conference on Cultural Policies held by UNESCO (Mexico City, 1982) adopted an authoritative international definition:
> "Culture should be regarded as the set of distinctive spiritual, material, intellectual and emotional features of society or a social group... it encompasses, in addition to art and literature, lifestyles, ways of living together, value systems, traditions and beliefs."

### 2.7 Culturology as an Independent Science: Leslie Alvin White (1949)
Prominent American anthropologist **Leslie Alvin White (1900–1975)** in *The Science of Culture* (1949) coined and established the discipline of **Culturology** as an independent science:
- White demonstrated that culture constitutes a distinct, extra-somatic (non-biological) realm of phenomena operating according to its own structural laws.
- According to White, the fundamental driver of culture is humanity's unique **symboling capacity (*symboling*)** — the ability to assign arbitrary, abstract meanings to physical objects, sounds, and signs.

## 3. Lecture 1.2: Morphology of Culture: Subsystems, Methods & Theories

**Official AITU Lecture 1.2:** `TTsgvJlvZ78` ([Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TTsgvJlvZ78))  

### 3.1 Defining Morphology of Culture
**Morphology of culture** is the specialized branch of culturology that investigates the internal structural composition of culture as a systemic social phenomenon, the regularities of its element configurations, and the historical processes through which cultural forms are created, sustained, and transformed.

---

### 3.2 Three Major Morphological Subsystems:

```
                           ┌─────────────────────────┐
                           │  STRUCTURE OF CULTURE   │
                           └────────────┬────────────┘
                                        │
           ┌────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────┐
           ▼                            ▼                            ▼
┌─────────────────────────┐  ┌─────────────────────────┐  ┌─────────────────────────┐
│   MATERIAL SUBSYSTEM    │  │   SPIRITUAL SUBSYSTEM   │  │ SOCIAL/INSTITUTIONAL    │
│ Physical tools, yurts,  │  │ Science, religion,      │  │ Laws, family, rituals,  │
│ technology, architecture │  │ ethics, art, values     │  │ state institutions      │
└─────────────────────────┘  └─────────────────────────┘  └─────────────────────────┘
```

1. **Material Subsystem (*Material Subsystem*):** Physical objects created or altered by human labor, agricultural tools, industrial machinery, architectural structures (nomadic yurts, modern buildings), transportation, clothing, foodways, and digital hardware.
2. **Spiritual Subsystem (*Spiritual Subsystem*):** The domain of abstract ideas, scientific theories, religious doctrines, philosophical systems, ethical codes, aesthetic values, literature, and visual arts.
3. **Social/Institutional Subsystem (*Social/Institutional Subsystem*):** Rules governing human social interaction, family organization, legal codes, civic rituals, educational systems, and state institutions.

> ⚠️ **Critical Exam Requirement:**  
> Physical tools, machinery, and technical equipment (**tools and technologies**) belong STRICTLY to **material culture** and are NOT part of spiritual culture!

---

### 3.3 Research Methodologies: The Genetic Method (*Genetic Method*)
Culturology utilizes diverse analytical tools. The specialized scientific method that studies the **origin (genesis), historical roots, and evolutionary development of cultural forms** is known as the **Genetic Method (*Genetic Method*)**.

### 3.4 Sociobiological Meme Theory: Richard Dawkins (1976)
The theoretical framework explaining cultural transmission through **memes** — replicating units of cultural information passed between minds — introduced by evolutionary biologist Richard Dawkins in *The Selfish Gene* (1976), is categorized as a **Sociobiological Theory of Culture (*Sociobiological Theory*)**.

---

### 3.5 Five Essential Societal Functions of Culture:

1. **Humanistic / Educational Function:** Cultivating human intellectual and moral potential, transforming a biological human organism into a social personality.
2. **Communicative Function:** Facilitating social interaction by transmitting shared cultural codes and meanings across individuals and generations.
3. **Regulatory / Normative Function:** Preserving social stability through systems of laws, moral codes, folkways, and manners (*Folkways & Mores*).
4. **Epistemological / Cognitive Function:** Organizing human knowledge about the universe, structuring worldviews, and providing frameworks for interpretation.
5. **Identificatory / Socializing Function:** Integrating individuals into social groups, fostering group cohesion, and instilling national or ethnic identity.

## 4. Seminar 1.3: 5 Core Building Blocks of Culture & Society Distinction

### 4.1 Five Core Building Blocks of Culture:

```
┌─────────┐      ┌─────────┐       ┌─────────┐      ┌─────────┐      ┌─────────┐
│ VALUES  │      │ BELIEFS │       │  NORMS  │      │ SYMBOLS │      │LANGUAGE │
│ (Values)│      │(Beliefs)│       │ (Norms) │      │(Symbols)│      │(Language│
└─────────┘      └─────────┘       └─────────┘      └─────────┘      └─────────┘
```

1. **Values:** Abstract, socially approved standards of what is desirable, good, right, and beautiful in a society.
2. **Beliefs:** Specific statements or convictions that people hold to be true (religious doctrines, scientific consensus).
3. **Norms:** Established rules of conduct. Categorized into:
   - **Folkways:** Informal customs and social etiquette. Violations lead to mild social awkwardness (e.g., wearing shoes inside a home).
   - **Mores:** Strict moral rules and taboos essential to group survival. Violations incur severe legal punishment or social ostracism (e.g., theft, treason, murder).
4. **Symbols:** Objects, marks, gestures, or words carrying recognized shared meanings (national flags, coats of arms).
5. **Language:** The primary symbolic system enabling **cumulative cultural transmission** (storing and passing accumulated knowledge across generations).

### 4.2 Sociological Distinction: Society vs. Culture

| Concept | Definition | Computing Analogy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Society (*Общество*)** | An organized group of people living in a defined territory interacting through social relationships. | **Hardware** |
| **Culture (*Культура*)** | The shared blueprint, values, norms, and language that guide their collective way of life. | **Software** |

### 5.3 Deep Semiotic Analysis of an Artifact: Kazakh Yurt (*Kiiz ui*)

The traditional Kazakh yurt provides a prime illustration of how a physical material artifact is imbued with non-material meanings:

- **Material Structure:** Wooden expandable walls (*Kerege*), roof poles (*Uyk*), central crown ring (*Shanyraq*), pressed felt coverings (*Syrmak*, *Tekemet*).
- **Non-Material Symbolic Meanings:**
  - **Shanyraq:** Serves as a sacred symbol of family lineage, cosmic order, home hearth, and peaceful unity in the State Emblem of Kazakhstan.
  - **Spatial Orientation:** The yurt door traditionally faces East to greet the rising sun, reflecting ancient Eurasian solar beliefs.

### 5.4 Cultural Lag Theory (*Cultural Lag*)

- **Theoretical Originator:** William Fielding Ogburn (*Social Change*, 1922).
- **Core Concept:** Material technology and physical inventions advance at a rapid pace, whereas non-material culture (laws, moral values, ethics, social institutions) adapts much slower, creating social tension and ethical confusion.
- **Exam Case Study:** The rapid deployment of Generative Artificial Intelligence tools (material technology) outpaces legal frameworks for copyright, academic integrity, and AI governance (non-material cultural lag).

## 6. Seminar 1.5: Typology of Cultural Forms (Crash Course Sociology #11)

**Video Source:** Task 1(c) — *Crash Course Sociology #11* ([Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RV50AV7-Iwc))  

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                           DOMINANT CULTURE                             │
│         (Mainstream patterns, state institutions, majority values)     │
└───────────────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────┘
                                    │
           ┌────────────────────────┴────────────────────────┐
           ▼                                                 ▼
┌───────────────────────────┐                     ┌───────────────────────────┐
│        SUBCULTURE         │                     │       COUNTERCULTURE      │
│ Shares baseline rules but │                     │ Actively rejects & seeks  │
│ uses distinct style/jargon│                     │ to replace dominant norms │
└───────────────────────────┘                     └───────────────────────────┘
```

1. **Dominant Culture (*Dominant Culture*):** The mainstream cultural patterns supported by the societal majority and dominant state institutions.
2. **High Culture (*High Culture*) vs. Popular Culture (*Popular Culture*):** Elite artistic forms requiring specialized training (opera, classical ballet) vs. mass commercial entertainment (pop music, blockbusters, memes).
3. **Subculture (*Subculture*, Chris Barker 2012):** A cultural group within mainstream society maintaining distinct jargon, fashion, and values without rejecting dominant society (gamers, K-pop fandoms).
4. **Counterculture:** A group actively opposing and seeking to undermine dominant cultural foundations (1960s Hippies, Punk movement).
5. **Evaluative Attitudes:** Ethnocentrism (judging other cultures by one's own standards) vs. Cultural Relativism (evaluating a culture objectively within its own historical context).

### 7.1 AITU Lecture 1.3: Language as Social Memory Carrier
Language is not merely a tool for messaging; it is a complex semiotic encoding system storing collective cultural memory, worldview categories, and historical experience.

### 7.2 Sapir-Whorf Hypothesis (Linguistic Relativity):
- **Strong Version (Linguistic Determinism):** Language strictly dictates thought boundaries (Scientific consensus rejects this).
- **Soft Version (Linguistic Relativity):** Language structure directs habitual attention and cognitive categorization.

### 7.3 Boroditsky's Four Empirical Experiments (TED):
1. **Spatial Navigation (Kuuk Thaayorre):** Using absolute cardinal directions (North/South) instead of egocentric terms (left/right) develops an innate spatial orientation compass.
2. **Color Discrimination (Russian Language):** Having distinct words for light blue (*goluboy*) and dark blue (*siniy*) leads to faster EEG brain distinction of shade boundaries.
3. **Grammatical Gender (German vs. Spanish):** Describing a "bridge" as feminine in German (*die Brücke* — elegant, slender) vs. masculine in Spanish (*el puente* — strong, sturdy).
4. **Memory for Blame (English vs. Spanish):** English focuses on agentive blame (*"John broke the vase"*), whereas Spanish emphasizes event occurrence (*"The vase broke itself"*).

### 7.4 Saussurean Semiotics:
A sign consists of two inseparable parts: **Signifier (*Signifier* — acoustic/visual form)** and **Signified (*Signified* — mental concept)**.

#### ❓ Q1: Trace the etymology of "culture" and Cicero's contribution.
**Answer:**  
The word originates from Latin *colere* (tilling soil). The original phrase *agri cultura* meant agriculture (*cultivation*). Marcus Tullius Cicero in 45 BCE (*Tusculan Disputations*) coined *Cultura Animi* ("cultivation of the soul"), arguing philosophy cultivates the human mind as agriculture enriches soil.

#### ❓ Q2: What was Leslie White's contribution to Culturology?
**Answer:**  
In 1949 (*The Science of Culture*), White established Culturology as an independent science studying extra-somatic phenomena grounded in human symboling capacity (*symboling*).

#### ❓ Q3: Detail the 3 morphological subsystems from AITU Lecture 1.2.
**Answer:**  
1. Material (physical tools, artifacts, yurts), 2. Spiritual (ideas, religion, ethics, values), 3. Social/Institutional (family, laws, state rituals).

#### ❓ Q4: List 5 core functions of culture.
**Answer:**  
1. Humanistic/Educational, 2. Communicative, 3. Regulatory/Normative, 4. Epistemological/Cognitive, 5. Identificatory/Socializing.

#### ❓ Q5: Differentiate Folkways from Mores.
**Answer:**  
Folkways are informal etiquette customs with mild sanctions. Mores are moral laws and taboos essential for group survival with severe sanctions.

#### ❓ Q6: Explain Society vs. Culture using a computing analogy.
**Answer:**  
Society is the population interacting in a territory (Hardware); Culture is the lifestyle program and rules (Software).

#### ❓ Q7: What is Ogburn's Cultural Lag?
**Answer:**  
The gap when material technology advances faster than non-material laws, morals, and social ethics.

#### ❓ Q8: What does soft Linguistic Relativity state?
**Answer:**  
Language structure does not strictly force thought, but directs habitual cognitive attention.

#### ❓ Q9: What are the two components of Saussure's sign?
**Answer:**  
Signifier (physical acoustic/visual form) and Signified (mental concept).

#### ❓ Q10: Define Uzelac's Participatory Culture.
**Answer:**  
A digital environment where consumers act as prosumers creating, editing, and sharing content.
