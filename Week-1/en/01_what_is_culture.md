## 🎯 Essence in 60 Seconds
- **Etymology:** Traces back to Latin *colere* (to cultivate soil). Literal meaning of *agri cultura* is agriculture / soil cultivation.
- **Philosophical Shift:** Marcus Tullius Cicero (45 BCE, *Tusculan Disputations*) coined the metaphor *Cultura Animi Virtus Est* ("Philosophy is the cultivation of the soul").
- **Anthropology:** Sir Edward Burnett Tylor (1871) formulated the first academic definition of culture as that complex whole of knowledge, beliefs, art, morals, laws, and customs acquired in society.
- **Behavioral Concept:** A. Kroeber & C. Kluckhohn (1952) defined culture as "shared, learned human behavior, a way of life."
- **Culturology:** Leslie Alvin White (1949) established *Culturology* as an independent science studying extra-somatic laws driven by human *symboling* capacity.
- **Morphology:** Culture comprises 3 subsystems: Material (tools, yurts, tech), Spiritual (ideas, religion, ethics), and Social-Institutional (laws, family).

## 📖 Main Content

### 1. Ancient Roman Etymology & Agronomic Roots
**The word "culture" originally designated the practical agronomic process of human labor transforming nature.**
The term originates from the Latin verb **colere** (*to cultivate, till, care for*). The literal meaning of **agri cultura** was **cultivation of the soil / agriculture (*cultivation*)**. In antiquity, culture did not refer to fine arts, but strictly to agricultural intervention in raw nature. Soil left untouched remained wild (*silva*); tilled soil became *cultura*.
**Example:** Growing grain on tilled land in ancient Rome represented the primary act of culture.

### 2. Cicero's Philosophical Breakthrough: Cultura Animi (45 BCE)
**Marcus Tullius Cicero transitioned the concept of culture from agriculture into the human spirit and philosophy.**
In *Tusculan Disputations* (45 BCE), Cicero formulated the enduring metaphor **Cultura Animi Virtus Est** (*"Philosophy is the cultivation of the soul"*). Cicero compared the uneducated mind to uncultivated land: just as fertile soil yields no harvest without tilling, so the human soul remains crude without cultivation through philosophy and science.
**Example:** Educating a student in ethics and science transforms their mind just as a plow transforms a field.

### 3. The Enlightenment & J.G. Herder
**In the 18th century, Enlightenment philosophers linked culture to human reason, while Herder established cultural pluralism.**
Voltaire, Montesquieu, and Rousseau viewed culture as social progress overcoming barbarism. Johann Gottfried Herder in *Ideas on the Philosophy of the History of Mankind* (1784–1791) first used "cultures" in the plural (*Kulturen*), arguing that every nation possesses its own unique culture.
**Example:** Eurasian nomadic culture possesses unique value equal to European urban culture.

### 4. Tylor's Anthropological Definition (1871)
**Sir Edward Burnett Tylor formulated the first academic definition of culture as an extra-somatic complex whole.**
In *Primitive Culture* (1871), Tylor wrote: *"Culture, or Civilization, is that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, law, morals, custom, and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society."* Culture is non-biological and learned through enculturation (*enculturation*).
**Example:** A child acquires language and etiquette from parents and society, rather than inheriting them genetically.

### 5. Kroeber & Kluckhohn (1952) and White's Culturology (1949)
**Leslie White established Culturology as an independent science grounded in humanity's symboling capacity.**
A. Kroeber and C. Kluckhohn (1952) established the behavioral definition of culture as *"shared, learned human behavior, a way of life."* Leslie Alvin White (1949, *The Science of Culture*) coined **Culturology** (*Culturology*), proving culture operates by extra-somatic laws through human *symboling*.

### 6. Three Morphological Subsystems of Culture (AITU 1.2)
**Culture consists of three structural subsystems: material, spiritual, and social-institutional.**
1. **Material Subsystem:** Physical artifacts, production tools, housing (yurts), clothing, technology, and transport. *(NOTE: Physical tools and technologies belong STRICTLY to material culture!)*
2. **Spiritual Subsystem:** Science, religion, philosophy, ethics, values, literature, and art.
3. **Social-Institutional Subsystem:** Laws, family institutions, state norms, rituals.
**Example:** The Kazakh yurt as a wooden-felt physical frame belongs to the material subsystem.

## ⚖️ Common Misconceptions

| Concept A | Concept B | Key Difference | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Material Culture** | **Spiritual Culture** | Tangible physical items vs non-material ideas and values. | Tools and AI technology belong STRICTLY to material culture! |
| **Society (Общество)** | **Culture (Культура)** | Group of interacting people (Hardware) vs their rule blueprint (Software). | Kazakhstan population = Society; language & traditions = Culture. |
| **Ethnocentrism** | **Cultural Relativism** | Judging another culture by one's own bias vs objective analysis in context. | Calling nomadic life "primitive" = Ethnocentrism. |
