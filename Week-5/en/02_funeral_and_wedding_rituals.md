## 🎯 Essence in 60 Seconds
- **A wedding is a contract between two clans, not two people.** Hence the chain: quda tusu → syrga salu → qalyng mal → uryn baru → qyz uzatu → kelin tusiru → betashar.
- **Burial:** estirtu → qoshtasu → zhoqtau → zhanaza → interment according to Muslim rite.
- **The memorial cycle:** ushisi (3rd day), zhetisi (7th), qyrqy (40th), zhyly (one year); the great memorial feast is the **as**.
- **What the rites say about belief:** death is not a rupture but a passage into the status of **aruaq**; ancestors continue to protect the clan.
- **The influence of Islam:** the zhanaza, burial in the earth facing Mecca, abandonment of earlier burial forms — while the cult of ancestors survived and fused with the veneration of saints.

## 📖 Main Content

### 1. The Wedding Cycle: From Matchmaking to Entering the Home
**A Kazakh wedding was not a single celebration but a long procedure in which each step consolidated the contract between clans.**
- **Quda tusu** — matchmaking. Envoys (quda) from the groom's clan negotiated with the bride's. The parties became in-laws (quda) permanently, and that bond survived the wedding itself.
- **Syrga salu** — "placing the earrings": the groom's mother put earrings on the bride as a sign that consent had been reached. This was the public mark of engagement.
- **Qalyng mal** — the marriage gift from the groom's clan to the bride's, usually in livestock. Its logic matters: this was not "buying a bride" but compensation to a clan losing a worker and simultaneously a guarantee of serious intent; the bride's side in turn provided a dowry (**zhasau**).
- **Uryn baru** — the groom's first visit to the bride before the wedding, staged with playful trials and ransoms demanded by the bride's friends.
- **Qyz uzatu** — the sending-off of the bride from her father's house. It is here that the song **zhar-zhar** is sung, the groom's side offering consolation while the bride's side laments.
- **Kelin tusiru** — the reception of the daughter-in-law at the groom's home, with a shower of sweets (**shashu**) for good fortune.
- **Otqa mai quyu** — "pouring oil on the fire": the bride approached the hearth of her new home for the first time, signifying her acceptance into the family.
- **Betashar** — the unveiling of the bride's face. A singer enumerated the husband's kin, and the bride bowed at each name. This was at once ceremony and practical instruction: the bride learned who was who in her new clan.
**Example:** the betashar is a model semiotic text: the bow functions as a sign acknowledging seniority, and the order in which names are recited encodes the structure of the clan.

### 2. What Wedding Rituals Say About Values and Social Organisation
**A wedding reveals three things: the priority of the clan, the distribution of gender roles, and the rules of kinship.**
First, the decision to marry was made by elders, because marriage touched the interests of the whole clan rather than of two individuals. Second, the rites distributed roles explicitly: the bride entered her husband's clan and assumed its hearth and obligations, which otqa mai quyu and the betashar formalised. Third, marriage was prohibited between relatives within seven generations — the rule of **jeti ata**, which made precise genealogical knowledge necessary.
**Example:** jeti ata is not an abstract tradition but a working mechanism: knowing seven ancestors was a practical precondition for marriage.

### 3. Funeral Rites
**Burial was organised so that grief would be shared by the community rather than left to the family.**
The order: **estirtu**, the careful announcement of death by allegory or song; **qoshtasu**, the farewell; **zhoqtau**, the women's lament enumerating the virtues of the deceased; **zhanaza**, the Islamic funeral prayer; then interment. The body was washed, wrapped in a shroud (**kebin**), and buried in the earth facing Mecca. Over the graves of people of standing a **kumbez** or mazar was raised — the domed structure discussed in [Week 4](../../Week-4/en/03_turkic_architectural_heritage.md).

### 4. The Memorial Cycle
**Memorials are spread over time because the passage of the deceased into the status of ancestor was considered gradual.**
The dates observed were **ushisi** (third day), **zhetisi** (seventh), **qyrqy** (fortieth), and **zhyly** (the anniversary). The great memorial for a person of standing was the **as**: clans gathered from across the district for **baige** (horse races), wrestling contests, and **aitys** between akyns. The year of mourning then closed, and the widow could remove her mourning dress.
**Example:** an as for a person of standing also served a political function: the gathering of clans was an occasion to settle disputes, negotiate, and confirm alliances.

### 5. What the Rites Say About Belief: Life, Death, and Ancestors
**Funeral rites express directly the conviction that the clan continues beyond the boundary of death.**
The deceased becomes an **aruaq**, a protecting spirit to whom one turns for support; an oath sworn on the ancestors' name was held to be the strongest of all. Hence the duty to tend graves, to remember seven generations, and never to disgrace the clan's name. Significantly, what was feared was not the dead person but the clan's forgetting: oblivion severed the bond.

### 6. The Influence of Islam
**Islam changed the form of funeral rites but did not displace the cult of ancestors — it merged with it.**
Islamic in origin are the **zhanaza**, the washing and the shroud, burial in the earth facing Mecca, the recitation of the Qur'an and the presence of a mullah, and the abandonment of earlier burial with grave goods. Pre-Islamic elements nevertheless survived: the zhoqtau, the memorial cycle, and above all the veneration of ancestors, which fused with the veneration of Muslim saints, the **auliye**, discussed in [Week 4 on Sufism](../../Week-4/en/04_sufism_anatolia_and_central_asia.md). This is why pilgrimage to a saint's grave and visits to ancestral graves came to stand side by side in popular practice.

## ⚖️ Common Misconceptions

| Concept A | Concept B | Key Difference | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Qyz uzatu** | **Kelin tusiru** | Sending the bride from her home vs receiving her in the groom's home. | Zhar-zhar at uzatu, betashar at tusiru. |
| **Qalyng mal** | **Zhasau** | Gift from the groom's clan to the bride's vs dowry from the bride's side. | The exchange runs both ways. |
| **Zhetisi** | **Qyrqy** | Memorial on the 7th day vs on the 40th day. | The count: 3 → 7 → 40 → one year. |
| **Zhoqtau** | **Zhar-zhar** | Lament for the dead vs wedding song at the bride's send-off. | Funeral vs wedding. |

## 🧠 Memory Aids
- **The wedding step by step:** matchmaking → earrings → marriage gift → visit → send-off → reception → betashar.
- **The funeral step by step:** announced → farewelled → lamented → prayed over → buried.
- **Memorials:** 3 → 7 → 40 → one year, with the greatest being the **as** with its horse races.

## ✅ Self-Check Quiz

<details><summary>1. Name the main stages of the Kazakh wedding cycle.</summary>
Quda tusu (matchmaking), syrga salu (engagement), qalyng mal (marriage gift), uryn baru (the groom's first visit), qyz uzatu (sending off the bride), kelin tusiru (receiving the daughter-in-law), and betashar (the unveiling).
</details>

<details><summary>2. What do wedding rituals reveal about social organisation?</summary>
That marriage was a contract between two clans rather than a private matter: elders made the decision, roles were assigned ritually (the bride entered her husband's clan), and the jeti ata prohibition on marriage within seven generations required precise genealogical knowledge.
</details>

<details><summary>3. What is the order of the funeral rites?</summary>
Estirtu (careful announcement of the death), qoshtasu (farewell), zhoqtau (lament), zhanaza (the Islamic funeral prayer), then washing, shrouding, and burial in the earth facing Mecca.
</details>

<details><summary>4. What do funeral rites say about conceptions of life and death?</summary>
That death does not sever the bond with the clan: the deceased becomes an aruaq, a protecting spirit. Hence the tending of graves, the memory of seven generations, and the conviction that what is dreadful is not death but oblivion.
</details>

<details><summary>5. How did Islam influence these rituals?</summary>
It introduced the zhanaza, the washing and shroud, burial facing Mecca, Qur'anic recitation, and the mullah's participation. Pre-Islamic elements nevertheless survived: the zhoqtau, the memorial cycle, and the cult of ancestors, which fused with the veneration of Muslim auliye saints.
</details>
